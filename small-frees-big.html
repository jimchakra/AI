Small Frees Big

What real-time RAN silicon taught me about where AI inference architecture is heading

Systems & heterogeneous silicon · inference at the memory boundary

Rare earth elements aren’t actually rare. Cerium is more common in the Earth’s crust than copper; most of the lanthanides are unremarkable. What makes them expensive is that they never occur concentrated. You mine enormous volumes of ordinary rock, haul it, crush it, and run it through long chemical separation trains — and the payload is a rounding error. At one representative deposit, roughly three million tons of ore are processed a year to yield about four thousand tons of rare-earth oxide: an ore grade near 0.13%, or some 750 tons of rock handled for every ton of product. Then, because the separation chemistry is the genuinely hard part, the concentrate is often shipped across the planet to be refined — China runs about 90% of the world’s rare-earth processing. The element was never the cost. Moving and refining the bulk around it is the entire bill.

Modern AI inference runs on the same economics, and we mostly don’t say so out loud. The multiply-accumulate at the heart of a neural network is almost free: on a current 5-nanometer-class process a low-precision MAC costs a fraction of a picojoule — the raw arithmetic is only tens of femtojoules. Fetching the operands it consumes is where the energy goes. A read from HBM runs a few picojoules per bit — tens of picojoules per byte — and from off-package DRAM closer to 640 picojoules for a single 32-bit word. Moving the number costs hundreds to thousands of times more than using it. And the gap is widening, not closing: every process node makes the arithmetic cheaper, while an off-chip fetch barely improves, because that cost is set by the physics of driving bits across a package, not by transistor density. Scale it up and transport swallows the budget — across a representative set of Google’s consumer workloads, 62.7% of total system energy went not to computation but to moving data between memory and the compute units. And that is a conservative, mixed-workload figure: it is held down by caches and reuse doing their job. For memory-bound LLM decode — where each weight is fetched from memory and used in a single multiply before the next token needs it again — there is almost no reuse to hide behind, and the movement share runs far higher, above ninety percent. (A companion energy model bears this out.) DRAM wins on density, so that is where the weights and the KV cache live, and so that is the rock we truck back and forth on every token.

Here’s the irony. We borrow the brain as inspiration for almost everything in machine learning — but this is the one place the metaphor quietly breaks, and breaks expensively. The brain has no memory bus. There is no separate storage region shuttling values off to a distant arithmetic unit; memory and computation are the same tissue, co-located down to the synapse. The von Neumann split between where you keep the numbers and where you do the math — the very thing costing us two-thirds of our energy budget — is an artifact of how we build machines, not of how intelligence has ever actually run.

Most conversations about AI accelerators start — and often end — at the big engine: how many TOPS, how wide the systolic array, which numeric format. That engine matters, but after years designing baseband silicon — user-equipment modems, and later radio access networks — I’ve come to believe it’s the easy part. The engineering that actually decides whether a system hits its deadlines and its power budget lives in the small, unglamorous compute around the big engine. And the shape of that surrounding system is turning out to be the same shape I kept building for RAN.

So the essay has a single argument, in two moves. The first is the one the title makes: the small, targeted compute around the big engine is the hero — it is what lets the throughput engine spend its cycles only on what differentiates. The second is where that rare-earth bill comes back: the move that matters next is to push some of that small compute, in some form, out toward the memory where the data lives — so it can reduce, gate, and filter the stream before anyone pays to move it. Small frees big; and small, relocated to the data, frees big from its largest bill.

Let me explain the pattern, and why I think AI inference silicon is converging on it.

The pattern I kept building

A real-time RAN datapath is not one big processor. It’s a heterogeneous, deadline-partitioned system. There is a hard-real-time tier — small cores paired with targeted fixed-function coprocessors — that handles the work with unforgiving microsecond deadlines: channel estimation, equalization, decoding, the things that must finish inside a slot or the air interface breaks. And there is a second tier for the heavy, soft-deadline, increasingly AI-driven work — scheduling, beam prediction, channel-state compression — that can run a slot or two ahead and tolerate jitter.

The organizing principle across all of it is simple to state and hard to do well: small, targeted compute close to the work frees the big engine to do only what differentiates. You don’t ask the throughput engine to spend its cycles on fixed-function housekeeping it will always be mediocre at; you spin out a cheap, specialized unit for that, and you let the expensive engine concentrate on the part that actually needs its generality. Wrapping it all is a QoS-aware scheduler that dispatches by deadline and priority — latency-critical first, throughput next, background last — so the tiers never fight over the same cycles at the wrong moment.

The economics are stark when you measure them. In my own RAN architecture work, a fixed-function LDPC decode engine comes in around 0.1 mm² at 5 nm, against roughly 1.5 mm² and 5–10 W for a general GPU streaming-multiprocessor doing the same decode — and the small unit is 16× faster on the task than software on the big engine. That is “small frees big” not as a slogan but as an area-and-power line item. (Worked through, with the contention and priority-inversion analysis behind it, in the 6G RU/DU silicon study.)

None of that was designed for AI. But look at where inference silicon is going.

The same shape, in AI inference

Control-plane small cores plus a big compute engine is already the standard accelerator pattern. Modern NPUs pair a large tensor/matmul engine with small scalar and vector units that orchestrate dataflow, DMA, and control. That’s the same split as a real-time core driving a fixed-function coprocessor — the labels differ, the topology doesn’t.

The latency-vs-throughput tiering is now the central problem in LLM serving. Interactive decode is latency-critical and largely memory-bound; prefill and batch work are throughput-bound and want to be packed. The field is actively splitting these onto different resources — “disaggregated serving” puts latency-sensitive and throughput-heavy phases on separate hardware pools, scheduled against service-level objectives. That is my hard-real-time tier and my soft-deadline tier, rediscovered under new names. If anything, the RAN version is more mature: we’ve been enforcing microsecond deadlines with jitter budgets and priority-inversion analysis for years, while much of the AI-serving world is only now learning to take latency SLOs seriously.

Small-fast-plus-big-heavy even shows up at the model level. Speculative decoding runs a small, fast draft model to propose tokens and a big model to verify them — “the many small” feeding “the one big,” applied to models instead of cores. Same instinct, different layer of the stack.

Where it points: two frontiers at the memory boundary

Here’s the part I find most interesting. If the principle is small targeted compute close to the work frees the big engine, then the logical endpoint is to keep pushing that small tier toward the data — because in inference the data movement, not the arithmetic, is what dominates both latency and energy. Reading operands across the package costs orders of magnitude more energy than the multiply that consumes them.

But “close to the data” has two answers, and the difference between them is most of the argument. One camp keeps the working set in on-chip SRAM and scales out across many chips, so the compute rarely has to touch main memory at all — bandwidth-hungry DRAM becomes a backstop rather than the hot path. The other puts small compute on the memory side itself — the base logic die of a 3D stack — so that when data must live in DRAM, it can be reduced, gated, and filtered before it ever crosses the bus. The first bet is elegant, and where the model fits it is hard to beat. The trouble is that the two things growing fastest in modern inference — long-context KV cache and frontier-scale weights — are precisely what overflows SRAM and spills back into main memory. That spill is structural, not incidental, and it is exactly the regime the SRAM bet does not cover.

Which surfaces a detail that’s easy to miss: conditional execution — skipping the zeros, gating the outputs that won’t survive an activation function — only actually saves energy if you evaluate the condition where the data lives. Move a value across the bus to a compute die only to discover it’s below threshold and discard it, and you’ve already paid the entire transport bill the gating was supposed to save. So once the working set no longer fits in SRAM, the move is to complete enough of the reduction on the memory side to make the keep-or-discard decision there, and return only the survivors — compressed. It is the same “small frees big” move as my fixed-function RAN coprocessors, relocated from beside the compute to beside the memory. I think that migration continues, and that the interesting accelerator work over the next several years is less about the tensor array and more about this distributed, data-adjacent small tier — and the scheduler that governs it.

The ingredient AI silicon is missing

There’s a skill gap worth naming. Throughput-first, deadline-relaxed thinking is deep in the AI-accelerator world; hard-real-time discipline is not. Worst-case execution time, bounded jitter, priority inversion, provable deadline margins — these are second nature in RAN and scarce in AI serving, and they are precisely what latency-bound inference (interactive agents, edge, automotive, robotics, and AI-RAN itself) is going to demand. The tiered, QoS-partitioned architecture in the figure isn’t just a nice diagram; making it meet deadlines under load is a real-time-systems problem, and that’s a body of practice the AI world is currently importing the hard way.

The through-line

So: is a two-tier “small for quick jobs, big for heavy jobs” processor relevant to modern AI-ASIC thinking? It’s not just relevant — a heterogeneous, QoS-partitioned design with a big throughput engine fed by small, targeted, latency-aware compute is close to where inference silicon is landing. RAN got there first because the air interface never let us pretend the big engine was the whole story. The same forces — deadlines, data movement, energy — are now doing to AI inference what they did to baseband a decade ago.

The big engine is the easy part. The architecture is the small compute around it — and, increasingly, the small compute moved out to the data.
