AI Inference & Systems · Problem Statement + Literature Survey

The fabric is the next wall

AI at scale is a data-movement problem, not an arithmetic one. The cluster is a memory hierarchy, and its outer tiers — the interconnect — are becoming the binding constraint. This is a problem statement and a survey of the art, read through a communications architect's lens. It is not a result and makes no performance claim: the goal is to frame the question clearly and be honest about what is already solved versus open.

01The cluster is a memory hierarchy

A modern AI system is not one machine — it is a hierarchy of data-movement tiers, each roughly an order of magnitude slower and costlier per byte than the one inside it. The entire architecture is a fight to not cross the next boundary.

TierScopeFabricOrder-of-magnitude BWLatency
On-chip SRAMwithin a dieNoC~TB/s ×10sns
HBMGPU-local memorystacked DRAM~TB/s~100 ns
Scale-upGPU↔GPU, one domainNVLink / NVSwitch~TB/s (aggregate)sub-µs
Scale-outnode↔nodeInfiniBand / RoCE~tens of GB/s / NICµs+

Scale-up means making one machine bigger — adding GPUs inside a tightly-coupled NVLink domain (a DGX, or a GB200 NVL72 acting as one big GPU). Scale-out means adding machines — connecting nodes over a network. The jump from scale-up to scale-out is the steepest cliff in the hierarchy, and it is exactly where large-model training and disaggregated inference are forced to live. That cliff is the wall this piece is about.

02Collectives and the barrier

Distributed AI communicates through collective operations — all-reduce, all-gather, reduce-scatter, all-to-all — implemented on GPU by NCCL. The defining property, for our purposes, is that most of them are barrier-synchronized.

Synchronous data-parallel training is bulk-synchronous: compute gradients → all-reduce them → update → repeat. The all-reduce is a barrier — every participant must contribute and receive the summed result before anyone proceeds, so its completion time is set by the slowest participant (max-over-participants). Tensor parallelism fires the same barrier many times per token, which is why it is kept inside the low-latency scale-up domain. Pipeline parallelism replaces the global barrier with producer–consumer hand-offs whose idle gaps are called bubbles. Disaggregated inference adds another hand-off: a prefill GPU must finish and transfer the KV cache before decode can start.

03The observation (a hypothesis, not a claim)

Put barrier-synchronized collective traffic and independent traffic (KV-cache transfers, disaggregated prefill→decode) on one contended fabric, and a tension appears that per-flow fairness handles badly.

Because a collective completes at the max over participants, per-flow "fairness" — which lets independent traffic take an equal share of every link — is arguably the wrong objective for it: a small amount of fair-share contention on the critical path stalls the entire step. The reasoning suggests a two-part policy: (1) protect the barrier (schedule the long-pole flow ahead of traffic that has slack), and (2) for the approximable traffic, gate by value-per-cost — keep a transfer in proportion to its importance divided by its byte-hop cost, so under stress the far, low-value bytes are shed first. Inside a single collective you cannot drop a participant without stranding the barrier, so there the lever is uniform payload compression, not per-flow dropping.

This is a hypothesis worth testing, framed by a communications architect — not a demonstrated result. It is the datacenter-scale version of two ideas I have worked with in modem silicon: deadline/slack scheduling of a contended medium, and shedding the least-valuable load first under pressure (graceful degradation). Whether it is worth implementing over existing congestion control is an empirical question (§05, §06).

04What the art already does — an honest survey

The mechanisms here are mature, and it matters to say so plainly. The open part is narrower than "add QoS to the fabric."

CapabilityRepresentative artStatus
Programmed QoS tiers (scale-out)InfiniBand Service Levels / Virtual Lanes + VL arbitration tablesMature (≈20 yr). Mechanism exists.
In-network reduction of collectivesSHARP (reduce in the NVSwitch)Shipping. Moves fewer bytes by computing in the fabric.
Congestion control / load balancingDCQCN (RoCE); adaptive routing; Spectrum-XMature / active.
Collective schedulingThemis, Crux, vClosActive.
Congestion-adaptive gradient compressionNetSenseML, L-GRECO, Deep Gradient CompressionEstablished.
KV-aware / topology-aware movementMooncake, NetKV, DistServe, "Internet for the KV Cache"Active, 2024–2026.

SHARP deserves emphasis: it is the industry already doing "compute where the data is, move fewer bytes" — the same principle as near-memory reduction, one tier out. It is a proof point for the data-movement thesis, not a competitor to it. And crucially: the QoS mechanism (VL arbitration) has existed for two decades. What is not standard is a policy that is (a) barrier-aware — today's Service-Level assignment is static per-connection and has no notion that a flow is one participant in a synchronized collective — and (b) content-aware by value-per-byte-hop rather than by a fixed class. So the open slice is the policy and the cheap importance signal that would drive it, riding lanes that already exist.

Bounds, stated as loudly as the numbers. This is a first-order fluid model, not cycle-accurate and not a benchmark. The magnitudes are directional and workload-dependent; move the traffic mix and they move. The only thing the model is meant to show is the shape: barrier priority is a structural, quality-free win that grows with contention; compression is a separate knob that trades collective quality for speed; and value-per-cost sheds the cheap bytes first. I would not put any of these numbers in front of you as a result — the code (fabric_qos_sim.py) is published precisely so the assumptions are inspectable.

06What I do not yet know — the questions I would own

The honest gaps, which are the actual research:

• Ring vs. tree. The model uses a simplified barrier; a real ring all-reduce bottlenecks as the sum over steps of each step's slowest link, and tree/double-binary-tree differ again. Does the conclusion survive the real algorithm's structure?
• SHARP and adaptive routing. In-network reduction removes collective bytes from the fabric and adaptive routing spreads load — both mitigate the contention the argument relies on. How much residual effect is left to justify a policy change?
• The value signal. Value-per-cost needs a cheap, off-critical-path importance estimate for a transfer — the same problem as a memory-side KV selector, one tier out. Can it be computed without paying more than it saves?
• Versus existing CC. Does barrier-aware scheduling beat well-tuned DCQCN + adaptive routing enough to matter, or does congestion control already capture most of it?

Framing those four questions precisely, and knowing which are cheap to answer, is the part I am confident about.

07Why a communications architect sees it this way

A collective is a synchronization barrier across a distributed fabric. Barriers, deadline/slack scheduling of a shared medium, and graceful degradation under overload are the daily material of modem and RAN silicon — a UE→RU→DU→CU pipeline is a hierarchical distributed system with hard-real-time sync points. In that language, fair-sharing a fabric between collectives and independent traffic is priority inversion: low-priority, droppable traffic steals from high-priority, synchronized traffic, and the whole machine waits on the barrier. The observation in §03 is that framing applied one domain over. That is the lens; the measured answer is the work that would follow.
