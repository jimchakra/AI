Block float, rediscovered

The frontier inference numeric — OCP microscaling, MXFP4 — is block floating point, which comms and DSP silicon have shipped for fifty years. A translation from multi-precision vector datapaths to the DL tensor engine, one FFN tile mapped onto a real MAC, and where the win actually lands.

Every few years the accelerator world gives an old idea a new acronym and treats it as an invention. The current one is microscaling — the OCP MX formats, MXFP8 down to MXFP4 — pitched as the numeric that finally makes 4-bit inference work. It does work, and it matters. But strip the branding and MX is block floating point: a block of narrow values sharing one exponent. FFT engines, fixed-point receivers, and modem datapaths have done exactly this since the 1970s, for exactly the same reason — hold dynamic range with cheap mantissas by amortizing the exponent across a block. If you've built comms silicon, you didn't adjacent-learn this format. You shipped it.

One idea, four names

Integer quantization with a per-tensor scale, integer with a per-channel scale, MX with a per-32 scale, and classical block floating point with a per-frame exponent are the same mechanism at different granularity: share a scale over a block of narrow values, so the mantissas stay cheap and the block keeps its dynamic range. DL is simply pushing the block finer — 32 wide — because finer blocks track the outliers that wreck low-bit LLM quantization. That's a real and useful refinement. It is not a new kind of arithmetic.

And the block runs the other way, too. Push it down to size two and you reach the complex case: the real and imaginary parts of an I/Q sample sharing a single exponent — the PCFL formats in modem receivers. Because I and Q ride a common envelope, their dynamic range is correlated, so one exponent legitimately covers both — arguably a subtler shared-exponent scheme than MX's homogeneous 32-wide block, and one whose datapath (a complex multiply-accumulate under a shared exponent) you have already taped out. Same idea, at the tightest and most correlated block there is.

So when the accelerator world announced “microscaling,” it wasn’t a new numeric to me — it was a familiar one at an unfamiliar block size. I’ve shipped shared-exponent arithmetic three ways in silicon: per-channel integer scaling, block-float FFT datapaths, and complex I/Q shared exponents (PCFL, sharing an exponent across correlated components — the harder case). MXFP4 is the 32-wide version I simply hadn’t had reason to build yet. This part isn’t new to me.

What it costs — and the gap is a block-size knob

For completeness, the quality numbers — because the point isn't that 4-bit is free, it's that the loss is small and, tellingly, set by exactly the block choices above. NVIDIA's NVFP4 study (a 12B Nemotron-H trained on 10T tokens) holds the training-loss gap to FP8 under ~1% for most of the run and ~1.5% at the end, with MMLU-Pro essentially tied to FP8 — 62.58% vs 62.62%. Head-to-head at 8B / 1T tokens, NVFP4 lands ~1.5% above the BF16 reference and MXFP4 ~2.5%, and MXFP4 needs about 36% more tokens to reach the same convergence. On the inference side, direct-cast MXFP4 weight quantization loses more, but calibration and rotation methods (MR-GPTQ, block rotation) recover most of it to within a small MMLU gap of BF16.

The instructive part is why NVFP4 beats MXFP4. The two formats differ in exactly two parameters: block size — 16 vs 32 — and scale format — an E4M3 float vs an E8M0 power-of-two. Finer block and a higher-precision shared scale buy accuracy back. That is the entire block-float design space, restated in benchmark numbers: the quality of a shared-exponent format is a function of how many values share the exponent and how precisely that scale is expressed. It is the same tradeoff a receiver engineer makes choosing a block length and a scale word for an FFT — dynamic range against bits spent on the exponent. The accelerator field has simply started measuring it on MMLU instead of on error-vector magnitude.

The datapath is the one you already built

An MX MAC is a block-float MAC, which is your multi-precision vector MAC with the exponent handled once per block instead of once per element. Multiply the narrow element mantissas in a small integer multiplier — a 4-bit×4-bit unit, cheaper than the int8×int4 tile you've already taped out. Accumulate wide across the 32-element block. Apply the two shared scales (activation-block × weight-block) as a single shift/multiply at the block boundary — amortized over 32 MACs, so its cost rounds to nothing. Requantize the result into the next block's format. That is the entire arithmetic. The SIMD/VLIW mixed-precision units, the requantization stages, the dynamic-range bookkeeping — you have designed all of it, under tighter power budgets than a datacenter part ever sees.

A worked tile — FFN down-projection at MXFP4

Take one output tile of an FFN down-projection: a 128-wide slab of the weight matrix in MXFP4, activations in MXFP8, feeding a 128×128 MAC array. Each PE does the 4-bit×(4/8-bit) multiply and accumulates in int32; every 32 elements along the contraction dimension, the two block scales are applied and the partial sums promoted to fp; the tile finishes with the SwiGLU non-linearity and requantizes its output block. The array is 16K MACs of 4-bit multipliers — a fraction of the area of an FP16 array of the same shape. Nothing here is unfamiliar if you have built a systolic decoder or a vector-engine GEMM: it is mixed-precision MACs, wide accumulation, and scale bookkeeping, which is the trade you already practice.

And here is the part that ties back to the rest of this work: at decode, the 4-bit format's biggest win is not the cheaper multiplier — the MAC was already sub-picojoule and the array already ~99% idle. It is that MXFP4 moves ~3.8× fewer bytes per weight than FP16 across the pins. Precision reduction is, first, a bandwidth lever.

That reframes the whole numerics question the way the memory-bound analysis reframes everything else. For compute-bound prefill, the narrow multiplier and the big regular GEMM array earn their keep — area and energy per FLOP fall with the format. For memory-bound decode, the format's value is that every weight and every KV element is fewer bytes to move, and the datapath that consumes them can be small because it is starved anyway. Same format, two entirely different reasons to want it, split exactly along the prefill/decode line.

Two levers, one budget — bits × survivors

Precision is only half of the byte budget. The bill for moving a tensor is values × bits-per-value, and those are two independent knobs. Block float shrinks the bits per value. The other lever — the survivor bitmap from the near-memory work — shrinks the number of values: gate the zeros an activation produces, or the KV pages a query won't attend to, and move only the survivors plus a bitmap that says which they were. The two compose, because one shrinks each value and the other deletes values, so the savings multiply.

Two honest notes keep this from drifting into a marketing multiplier. The block scale costs ~0.25 bit per value and the survivor bitmap costs one bit per position — and that bitmap bit is a floor that grows as a share of the total as sparsity rises, so past roughly 85–90% skipped you drop the bitmap for an index list, or the metadata swamps the savings. And the two levers are not perfectly free of each other: aggressive low precision and aggressive skipping both spend from the same quality budget, so you tune them together, not independently. With those caveats stated, the compound is real and large — roughly 5× on gated weights and ~9× on selected KV versus dense FP16 — and it is the same single idea the whole portfolio turns on: the pins are the bottleneck, so move fewer bytes, in fewer bits, for fewer values.

The one genuinely new thing — and how to hold it

What a vector engine or a channel decoder does not teach you is the specific craft of the large, regular GEMM array: choosing weight-stationary versus output-stationary versus row-stationary dataflow, and tiling a matmul so the array stays fed. But even that follows the prefill/decode split rather than sitting apart from it. Prefill is a big square GEMM — reuse is high, so a weight- or output-stationary array amortizes operand fetch and the systolic structure pays off. Decode is a tall-skinny GEMV — there is no weight reuse at batch one, the array runs at single-digit utilization, and the right answer is not a bigger array but a smaller datapath fed by more bandwidth, which is the decode-specialized-silicon argument from the system model. So the dataflow decision is not a separate discipline to acquire; it is the same operating-point reasoning applied one level down, at the array instead of the chip.

The honest summary, then, is not "learn compute datapath." It is that multi-precision arithmetic silicon — mixed-format MACs, wide accumulation, block-shared scaling, requantization under a power budget — is a craft comms and graphics have practiced for decades, and the DL frontier has walked back into it. The block-float lineage is not a gap to cover. On the numeric the field is currently most excited about, it is a head start.
