The digital twin, finally affordable

The methodology that avoids silicon re-spins always worked. It was just too expensive to always build. That is the thing AI coding agents actually change — and it reorders the economics of a chip program.

A leading-node premium SoC now costs on the order of a billion dollars to develop, and the largest line item by far is not masks, not EDA licenses, not IP — it is engineering labor, roughly two-thirds of the bill. Labor cost has a simple shape:

program cost ≈ heads × time × loaded-rate,  and  time ≈ base + (re-spins × cycle-time).

So the two things that move the number most are how many engineers you need and how long you keep them. And time, on a silicon project, is dominated by a discrete, brutal event: the re-spin. When a bug escapes verification into fabricated silicon, you pay for a new mask set — but that $20–30M is the small part. The real cost is that the entire team waits three-to-six months for the next wafers while continuing to burn, and the market window you were racing for moves without you. A single leading-node re-spin, fully loaded, is a meaningful fraction of the whole program. Re-spins are where budgets and schedules go to die.

I have spent most of my career attacking that one variable.

The cure nobody can afford to build

The cure for re-spins is not a secret. It is a pre-silicon digital twin: a single source of truth for the micro-architecture that fans out to the RTL, a cycle-accurate model, an instruction-accurate simulator running the real firmware, a golden reference, and a verification environment — all kept in lockstep, all exercised continuously, so that a spec change that would have drifted the RTL and the firmware apart instead fails a test the same afternoon. Build that, and first silicon bring-up becomes confirmation, not discovery. You have already run the chip — in simulation, against real software — thousands of times before the mask is cut.

This works. It has always worked. The reason most teams under-build it is that it is expensive. Standing up the model, the ISS, the golden reference, the register headers, the UVM environment, the CI that re-runs lint, simulation, clock-domain checks, synthesis, and logical-equivalence on every push — and then keeping all of it in sync as the spec moves under you — is a large, unglamorous, never-finished engineering cost. Under schedule pressure it is the first thing cut. Teams gamble on catching bugs later, and the gamble is a re-spin. The rigor that prevents the most expensive event in the program is itself treated as a luxury, because the plumbing to sustain it costs too much.

What AI actually changes

Here is the claim, stated narrowly on purpose: an AI coding agent does not design your chip, and it does not replace the architect’s judgment. What it does is collapse the cost of the twin’s plumbing — and that plumbing is exactly the part that made the re-spin-avoiding methodology unaffordable.

The unglamorous, high-volume work of building and maintaining a digital twin is where these agents are strongest: generating and extending testbenches and coverage; writing the golden reference model from the same spec that generates the RTL; producing register maps and firmware headers; scripting the flow and the CI; converting formats; cross-checking that the RTL, the model, the firmware, and the spec still agree; and — the part that used to rot — keeping those artifacts synchronized as the design changes. None of this is the crux of the chip. All of it is a large fraction of the hours, and all of it is now cheap.

The consequence is not a productivity footnote. It moves the methodology from “a discipline the best-funded teams can afford” to “the affordable default,” and it does so on all three factors of the cost identity at once: fewer heads to sustain the same rigor, shorter cycle-time per design-verify loop, and — because the verification is now more complete, not less — fewer re-spins. Because those multiply, even a modest improvement in each compounds into a large reduction in the whole. And the largest single term, re-spin avoidance, is precisely the one the twin was built to attack.

Why AI without the twin is a liability

This is the part the honest version of the argument has to include, and it is also why the two ideas are complements rather than substitutes. AI-generated RTL and firmware can carry subtle, confident-looking bugs — the same way a junior engineer’s can, sometimes in less obvious places. An agent pointed at a codebase with no golden reference, no coverage, and no continuous equivalence checking will happily accelerate you toward a re-spin. Speed without a safety net is just a faster way to fabricate the wrong chip.

The digital twin is that safety net. It catches the agent’s mistakes exactly as it catches a human’s — a failed equivalence check does not care who wrote the divergent line. So the relationship runs both ways: the methodology makes the agent safe to use, and the agent makes the methodology cheap to build. That loop is the actual mechanism. It is also why the crux work — the architecture, the analog and high-speed I/O, the physical sign-off, and the human who signs their name to the tape-out — stays human. AI does not remove the need for judgment; it removes the excuse for skipping the verification that protects it.

The receipts

I would not make this argument from a slide. I ran the experiment on myself. Over a few weeks of evenings and weekends around a full-time job — a handful of days of actual work — largely solo and with an AI coding agent (Claude Code), I took a near-memory gated-reduction inference datapath from a first-order energy thesis to a filed U.S. provisional patent, verified RTL, a single-source-of-truth flow, open continuous integration running lint / simulation / clock-domain crossing / synthesis / logical-equivalence on every push, a hardware-in-the-loop co-simulation that runs a real transformer tile through the actual RTL bit-exactly, and a validation of the core result on a real pretrained model. It is all public and reproducible.

That is an n of one, and I will not pretend otherwise — but it is a real, inspectable one, which is worth more than a productivity statistic. What it demonstrates is not that an agent can replace a design team. It is that one architect, holding the judgment and the sign-off, could stand up and sustain the entire re-spin-avoiding methodology — the twin, the CI, the equivalence checking — at a cost that would have been unthinkable for a single person a few years ago. That is the whole thesis, made concrete: the repository and the study.

Why this matters most where iteration is everything

Two honest boundaries. This is the digital and functional-verification side of the problem; analog, high-speed I/O, and process-driven re-spins need their own cures and are not what an agent catches today. And productivity gains vary widely with how much of the twin you already had. But the direction is not in doubt: the most expensive event in a silicon program is now cheaper to prevent, because the discipline that prevents it is finally cheap to run.

The teams that will feel this first are the ones for whom iteration speed is the product — custom AI-accelerator programs, where the differentiation is the silicon itself and the ability to close the design-verify-respin loop quickly is a competitive weapon, not an overhead. For twenty-five years the answer to “how do we avoid the re-spin” was “spend more on rigor than the other team can afford.” The answer is changing to “the rigor is affordable now — so there is no excuse not to.” That is a better world to design silicon in, and it rewards exactly the instinct the discipline was always supposed to reward: make bring-up a confirmation.
