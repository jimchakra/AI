The digital twin, finally affordable

The methodology that avoids silicon re-spins always worked. It was just too expensive to always build. That is the thing AI coding agents actually change — and it reorders the economics of a chip program. The same two-level twin buys a second payoff: a fast high-level model finds the design that bends instead of breaks past P99 — reliability with no added area, and roughly 2× capacity.

A leading-node premium SoC now costs on the order of a billion dollars to develop, and the largest line item by far is not masks, not EDA licenses, not IP — it is engineering labor, roughly two-thirds of the bill. Labor cost has a simple shape:

program cost ≈ heads × time × loaded-rate,  and  time ≈ base + (re-spins × cycle-time).

So the two things that move the number most are how many engineers you need and how long you keep them. And time, on a silicon project, is dominated by a discrete, brutal event: the re-spin. When a bug escapes verification into fabricated silicon, you pay for a new mask set — but that $20–30M is the small part. The real cost is that the entire team waits three-to-six months for the next wafers while continuing to burn, and the market window you were racing for moves without you. A single leading-node re-spin, fully loaded, is a meaningful fraction of the whole program. Re-spins are where budgets and schedules go to die.

I have spent most of my career attacking that one variable.

The cure nobody can afford to build

The cure for re-spins is not a secret. It is a pre-silicon digital twin: a single source of truth for the micro-architecture that fans out to the RTL, a cycle-accurate model, an instruction-accurate simulator running the real firmware, a golden reference, and a verification environment — all kept in lockstep, all exercised continuously, so that a spec change that would have drifted the RTL and the firmware apart instead fails a test the same afternoon. Build that, and first silicon bring-up becomes confirmation, not discovery. You have already run the chip — in simulation, against real software — thousands of times before the mask is cut.

This works. It has always worked. The reason most teams under-build it is that it is expensive. Standing up the model, the ISS, the golden reference, the register headers, the UVM environment, the CI that re-runs lint, simulation, clock-domain checks, synthesis, and logical-equivalence on every push — and then keeping all of it in sync as the spec moves under you — is a large, unglamorous, never-finished engineering cost. Under schedule pressure it is the first thing cut. Teams gamble on catching bugs later, and the gamble is a re-spin. The rigor that prevents the most expensive event in the program is itself treated as a luxury, because the plumbing to sustain it costs too much.

What AI actually changes

Here is the claim, stated narrowly on purpose: an AI coding agent does not design your chip, and it does not replace the architect’s judgment. What it does is collapse the cost of the twin’s plumbing — and that plumbing is exactly the part that made the re-spin-avoiding methodology unaffordable.

The unglamorous, high-volume work of building and maintaining a digital twin is where these agents are strongest: generating and extending testbenches and coverage; writing the golden reference model from the same spec that generates the RTL; producing register maps and firmware headers; scripting the flow and the CI; converting formats; cross-checking that the RTL, the model, the firmware, and the spec still agree; and — the part that used to rot — keeping those artifacts synchronized as the design changes. None of this is the crux of the chip. All of it is a large fraction of the hours, and all of it is now cheap.

The consequence is not a productivity footnote. It moves the methodology from “a discipline the best-funded teams can afford” to “the affordable default,” and it does so on all three factors of the cost identity at once: fewer heads to sustain the same rigor, shorter cycle-time per design-verify loop, and — because the verification is now more complete, not less — fewer re-spins. Because those multiply, even a modest improvement in each compounds into a large reduction in the whole. And the largest single term, re-spin avoidance, is precisely the one the twin was built to attack.

Why the twin is the ideal thing to hand an agent

There is a deeper reason this particular work suits an agent so well, and it is worth making explicit. RTL is a punishing multi-objective problem: the same code has to be functionally correct and meet timing and fit area and hit power and stay synthesizable and remain testable — objectives that actively fight one another. A HW/FW C model has exactly one objective: match the specified behavior. Nobody cares whether the model is slow or fat or inelegant; they care only that it computes the right answer. We deliberately do not model timing, power, or micro-architectural efficiency — only function.

The twin has one job: be right. Not fast, not small, not synthesizable — right. Collapsing six coupled constraints down to one is exactly where an agent goes from “assist” to “just do it.”

That single-objective nature is why it is perfectly fine for AI to write the twin — and to write it with a free hand. There is no timing to close, no floorplan to disturb, no synthesizable subset to respect, so the agent can use whatever abstraction is clearest and be given full generative freedom. In the language of the maturity-staged discipline, the twin sits at the “generate freely” end of the curve, not the ECO-constrained end: the two things that make late RTL dangerous to hand an agent — coupled objectives you must protect, and no independent check — are both absent here. The twin has one objective and it carries its own cheap oracle: directed tests against the spec, and a differential test against the RTL.

The one discipline that does survive is independence. A reference is only golden if its mistakes are uncorrelated with the RTL’s. Same spec is fine — the spec is the golden — but if one agent, one prompt, one misreading of an ambiguous line produces both the model and the RTL, they will agree with each other and be confidently wrong together, a silent false pass. So the twin is derived independently — a different prompt, seed, or engineer — and the written spec, owned by a human, stays the true reference both are validated against. Its functionality-only nature is a gift here too: a model you only have to reason about for behavior is far easier for a human to review than timing-critical RTL.

Two levels, two payoffs

The twin is really two models, and they buy two different things — which is why a second thesis belongs here rather than in its own silo.

The high-level model is fast, and that is the whole point. Because a simple Python model runs in milliseconds, you can Monte-Carlo it — thousands of stochastic runs over load and contention that a cycle-accurate simulator, orders of magnitude slower, could never afford. That statistical volume is how you characterize the tail instead of the average, and the tail is where complex chips actually fail: you cannot provision the P100, because it lives in contention on shared compute, not on the datapath, so no reasonable amount of silicon buys it. The move the high-level model reveals is to stop trying — build graceful degradation, a throttle, into the datapath so the same silicon bends instead of breaking past P99. It stays reliable well beyond the point a hard design collapses, with no added area, and the tail headroom you no longer have to provision converts into roughly 2× serving capacity, sold as tiered QoS. The full simulations are in bend, don’t break; the point here is that you can only find that trade with the high-level model — the low-level twin is far too slow to explore it.

The low-level twin buys the other payoff. It is too slow for Monte-Carlo, but it is cycle-accurate, so it is the model that pulls firmware and software forward — the team writes and debugs against it months before silicon exists — and it is the golden reference that turns first bring-up into confirmation, not discovery. That is the re-spin avoidance the rest of this piece is about.

So it is one methodology, two levels, two payoffs: capacity and reliability from the top, schedule and re-spin avoidance from the bottom. Both were historically too expensive to build and keep in sync; AI is what makes standing up both affordable at once — a statistical high-level explorer and a cycle-accurate low-level golden, each feeding the flow below.

The flow — and where trust bottoms out

Put end to end, the methodology is a pipeline with a trust gate on every arrow — the human owns the two endpoints and a low-bandwidth seed oracle, and an agent generates the volume in between. It also climbs a modeling ladder well before any RTL exists: first a fast high-level model — often a simple Python model that predicts throughput, the power/bandwidth envelope, the bottlenecks, and the behavioral trade-offs under load, such as throttling, admission control, and throughput against the latency tail (the kind of study in the degradation work) — and only then the cycle-accurate low-level twin that pins down the function. The two answer different questions — the high-level model is about performance and behavior, the twin about correctness — but neither is a product: nobody ships a model, so an agent can write both freely. RTL then comes in the middle, not last: you need its synthesis to get real timing and area, which feed into the architecture and many downstream decisions — and when it is differential-tested, the golden twin is already there to check it against.

One node in that diagram hides most of the work. “Human-owned spec” is not a file — it is a three-part contract, and the part everyone pictures is the smallest. First, the micro-architectural spec (MAS): registers, datapath, protocols — what the design does on legal input. Second, the project governance: naming, coding style, clocking / reset / CDC rules — the conventions that keep a thousand agent-generated artifacts in one dialect instead of a thousand. Third, and longest by far, the verification spec: what the design must do across the whole input space, including every way it can be exercised wrong. The MAS is a minority of the lines — stating intent is short; stating all the ways intent must not break is long.

There is a subtlety hiding in the word golden, and it is the deepest point in the whole flow. A model is not golden because an agent wrote it. It is golden only after it survives a thorough regression — and a regression needs expected answers, so someone has to say what “correct” means. If the same agent writes the model and the tests, a shared misreading of the spec makes them agree and both be wrong. This is the oracle bootstrap, the irreducible core of verification: you cannot manufacture certainty from nothing.

It does not dissolve; it terminates — at the third part of that contract. The verification spec is the human-owned artifact the regression is built from: the directed acceptance cases that define what “correct” means, the invariants, and — the part the MAS leaves open — the off-nominal decision for every illegal input (ignore, clamp, assert, safe-error, or don’t-care). That is the true golden. It is also what makes the differential test honest: two independent implementations agree on the specified space and may legally diverge on the don’t-cares, so “a mismatch is a bug” only holds once the verification spec has closed them — declared which divergences are illegal. Skip that step and the golden is noisy, flagging corners where neither side is wrong.

The agent can amplify this spec — it is genuinely good at enumerating what could go wrong — but a human owns and ratifies the decisions, and the spec stays independent of the implementation, or the correlated-error trap returns. Three things then buy confidence cheaply on top of it: independence — derive the twin and the RTL separately, so a bug has to fool both the same way to survive, exactly as safety-critical N-version practice has always required; metamorphic checks that need no golden output at all (decode of encode returns the input; the output is a permutation of the input; energy is conserved); and, where the spec cites a standard — an ISA, a bus, a codec, IEEE-754 — the external conformance suite the standards body already wrote and vetted.

So “golden” was never a proof. It always meant survived a diverse, adversarial, human-anchored battery until the residual-bug probability fell below the re-spin risk someone would sign for — as true when a human hand-wrote the twin as it is now. AI changes who writes the volume, not where trust bottoms out. Which is exactly why the scarce, high-value role migrates from writing code to authoring and owning the verification spec — the acceptance cases, the invariants, and the off-nominal decisions no implementation can infer — the one artifact that has to stay human-golden.

Why AI without the twin is a liability

This is the part the honest version of the argument has to include, and it is also why the two ideas are complements rather than substitutes. AI-generated RTL and firmware can carry subtle, confident-looking bugs — the same way a junior engineer’s can, sometimes in less obvious places. An agent pointed at a codebase with no golden reference, no coverage, and no continuous equivalence checking will happily accelerate you toward a re-spin. Speed without a safety net is just a faster way to fabricate the wrong chip.

The digital twin is that safety net. It catches the agent’s mistakes exactly as it catches a human’s — a failed equivalence check does not care who wrote the divergent line. So the relationship runs both ways: the methodology makes the agent safe to use, and the agent makes the methodology cheap to build. That loop is the actual mechanism. It is also why the crux work — the architecture, the analog and high-speed I/O, the physical sign-off, and the human who signs their name to the tape-out — stays human. AI does not remove the need for judgment; it removes the excuse for skipping the verification that protects it.

The receipts

I would not make this argument from a slide. I ran the experiment on myself. Over a few weeks of evenings and weekends around a full-time job — a handful of days of actual work — largely solo and with an AI coding agent (Claude Code), I took a near-memory gated-reduction inference datapath from a first-order energy thesis to a filed U.S. provisional patent, verified RTL, a single-source-of-truth flow, open continuous integration running lint / simulation / clock-domain crossing / synthesis / logical-equivalence on every push, a hardware-in-the-loop co-simulation that runs a real transformer tile through the actual RTL bit-exactly, and a validation of the core result on a real pretrained model. It is all public and reproducible.

That is an n of one, and I will not pretend otherwise — but it is a real, inspectable one, which is worth more than a productivity statistic. What it demonstrates is not that an agent can replace a design team. It is that one architect, holding the judgment and the sign-off, could stand up and sustain the entire re-spin-avoiding methodology — the twin, the CI, the equivalence checking — at a cost that would have been unthinkable for a single person a few years ago. That is the whole thesis, made concrete: the repository and the study.

Why this matters most where iteration is everything

Two honest boundaries. This is the digital and functional-verification side of the problem; analog, high-speed I/O, and process-driven re-spins need their own cures and are not what an agent catches today. And productivity gains vary widely with how much of the twin you already had. But the direction is not in doubt: the most expensive event in a silicon program is now cheaper to prevent, because the discipline that prevents it is finally cheap to run.

The teams that will feel this first are the ones for whom iteration speed is the product — custom AI-accelerator programs, where the differentiation is the silicon itself and the ability to close the design-verify-respin loop quickly is a competitive weapon, not an overhead. For twenty-five years the answer to “how do we avoid the re-spin” was “spend more on rigor than the other team can afford.” The answer is changing to “the rigor is affordable now — so there is no excuse not to.” That is a better world to design silicon in, and it rewards exactly the instinct the discipline was always supposed to reward: make bring-up a confirmation.
